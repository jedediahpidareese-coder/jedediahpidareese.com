一票

What One Vote Is Worth

Economists have an argument that a single vote is not worth casting. Here is that argument, built out of the certified results of every congressional and state legislative election on record.

Jedediah Pida-Reese

The argument goes like this. Your ballot changes who wins only if the election would otherwise come down to a single vote. In an electorate of millions that almost never happens. Voting takes time, learning enough to vote well takes much more time, and a tiny chance at a large prize is still worth less than the trip to the polling place. So a person who cares only about the result should stay home.

Most of that reasoning holds up. This page works it out instead of asserting it, using the Federal Election Commission's certified presidential returns, the Clerk of the House's district-by-district tallies, and an archive of state legislative returns covering 1968 to 2016. Nothing below is a stored answer. Every figure is computed in your browser from those returns, and you set the assumptions.

一 A question first

How many elections for Congress has one vote ever decided?

Since 1976 there have been 10,220 contested general elections for the United States House of Representatives. Pick the number you think were decided by exactly one vote.

None

Two came within ten votes. Indiana's 8th district finished four votes apart in 1984, out of 234,092 cast. Iowa's 2nd district finished six apart in 2020, out of 413,989. Eight races since 1976 finished within a hundred votes of each other.

State legislatures answer the same question differently. Among 82,590 contested elections for state house and state senate seats between 1968 and 2016, eleven finished in an exact tie and 26 were decided by a single vote. That is one race in roughly every 3,200.

The voters in the two settings are the same people. What differs is how many of them there are, and the rest of this page is about what that difference does.

四 The presidency

A presidential vote has to work twice

A vote for president never enters a national total. It goes into a state, the state hands every one of its electors to whoever wins it, and the electors are counted toward 270. One vote decides the presidency only when two things happen together: your own state finishes exactly tied, and your state is the one that pushes the winner past 270.

五 The rest of the ballot

One trip pays for every race on the paper

The argument is almost always run on the presidential race, which is the longest shot printed on the ballot. The same trip to the same polling place settles every other contest as well, and those electorates are far smaller.

Official ballot — the chance one vote decides each contest

A seat in a state legislature drew a median of 16,390 voters across 82,590 contested races between 1968 and 2016. A Wyoming state house seat drew a median of 3,158 voters across 340 contested races over the same years. Eleven of those 82,590 races ended in an exact tie.

Small elections decide large amounts of money. In April 2020 the Racine Unified School District in Wisconsin approved a billion dollars of additional revenue over thirty years by 16,715 votes to 16,711. Four votes settled it.

The popular version of this argument charges the whole cost of one trip against the presidential race and then reads the odds off that race alone. Divide the trip across the contests on the ballot and measure it against the shortest odds rather than the longest, and the comparison moves by three or four orders of magnitude.

六 The other side of the ledger

What voting costs you

Charles Stewart and Stephen Ansolabehere measured the 2012 election and found an average wait of 13.3 minutes, with 37.3 percent of voters waiting not at all. A study that tracked about 150,000 smartphones across more than 40,000 polling places in 2016 found a median wait of 14 minutes. At the median American hourly wage of $24.51 in May 2025, fourteen minutes of waiting costs about six dollars.

Leave the last control at zero and the verdict is the familiar one: a presidential vote returns a fraction of a cent against a cost of tens of dollars, and raising the value of the outcome to a million dollars still leaves it worth a few cents.

Move that control off zero and the verdict turns over for most of the contests on the ballot, for a reason Aaron Edlin, Andrew Gelman and Noah Kaplan published in 2007. A voter who counts other people at all is no longer valuing one outcome for himself. He is valuing it for everyone the election affects, and that number grows with the size of the country at the same rate the chance of deciding the election shrinks. The electorate appears once in the numerator and once in the denominator, and it cancels.

What survives is the ratio of people affected to people voting, which runs to about two or three and does not fall as the country grows. Their own worked case puts the social value of one vote near $250. Against a thirteen-minute wait worth about $5.25, voting pays for anyone who weighs a stranger's welfare at more than two or three percent of his own. A presidential vote in a safe state stays under water even then, because that first gate is shut whatever the voter cares about.

七 Why the conclusion does not follow

Three answers economists give

The arithmetic on this page is not in dispute. What it establishes is disputed, and three serious answers are on offer. None of them requires throwing the model away.

The electorate cancels

A voter who places any weight on other people is valuing the outcome for millions, not for himself. The benefit grows with the population as fast as the chance of deciding shrinks, so the two effects cancel and one vote is worth a few hundred dollars rather than a fraction of a cent.

Edlin, Gelman and Kaplan, 2007

Voting is not an investment

If the expected return on a vote in a national election is a tiny fraction of a cent, then whatever voters are doing, they are not investing. Voting on this account resembles cheering at a match: the expression is the point, and it is worth something whether or not it changes the score.

Brennan and Lomasky, 1993

Duty was in the equation all along

William Riker and Peter Ordeshook added a term for duty in 1968 precisely because the arithmetic could not explain the behavior. Critics call that relabeling rather than explaining. Whatever the term contains, it moves about two-thirds of American adults to the polls in a presidential year.

Riker and Ordeshook, 1968

What the arithmetic does establish

Three claims survive all of this. A single vote almost never changes a national outcome, and the record confirms it: none of the 10,220 contested House elections since 1976 was decided by one vote. The instrumental value of a vote depends enormously on which contest it is cast in, and a state legislative race is worth thousands of presidential races by this measure. And a person who votes for instrumental reasons alone, while counting nobody but himself, is doing something the arithmetic cannot justify.

What the arithmetic does not establish is that voting is irrational. The same calculation that makes a presidential vote worthless to a purely self-interested voter makes it worth a few hundred dollars to a voter who counts other people at all. This material is often taught as a debunking. It works better as a lesson in how much a conclusion can turn on an assumption nobody said out loud.

The model

What counts as decisive. This page reports the chance that the other voters finish exactly tied, so that one more ballot settles the result. Edlin, Gelman and Kaplan count a vote that creates a tie broken by lot as half a decision, which halves their figures against these.

The forecast model. The chance of a tie is the height of the forecast distribution at one half, divided by the number of voters. The simulation draws a vote share from a normal distribution centered on a dead heat, with the standard deviation set by the slider, and counts how close the resulting margin lands. The approximation treats the vote share as continuous, and it fails for electorates of a few hundred, where the exact combinatorial calculation belongs instead.

The electoral college. Twenty thousand simulated maps. Every state moves by a common national swing and then by an independent state shock, and each state's own shock is integrated analytically rather than sampled. Real state errors are correlated in ways this misses, and correlated errors concentrate decisiveness in fewer states than shown here. Maine and Nebraska award some electors by congressional district; the simulation treats both as winner-take-all.

The social term. Following Edlin, Gelman and Kaplan (2007), the outcome is worth $10 a head to people other than the voter. Two people are affected for every person who votes, which is a shade under the ratio in their own worked case and keeps the presidential figure below the population of the country. The slider sets how heavily the voter weighs a stranger against himself.

A tie is not a decisive vote. Recounts, litigation and statutory tie-breaking rules stand between a tied count and a result. Virginia's 94th House of Delegates district finished 11,608 apiece in 2017, and the winner was drawn from a bowl on 4 January 2018. No voter decided that election. A judge and a bowl did.
